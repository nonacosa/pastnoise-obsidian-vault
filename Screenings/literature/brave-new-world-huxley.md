# The Soma of Our Attention

> Huxley's prophecy was not about pain but pleasure—and the quiet erosion of the self.

## Core Thesis
Aldous Huxley's Brave New World is often shadowed by Orwell's boot, yet its true terror lies in the soft cage of comfort. As Tristan Harris notes, Huxley saw a world where people 'love their servitude'—where pleasure, not pain, becomes the tool of control. The novel's citizens trade freedom for stability, and in doing so, lose the very friction that makes us human. From Jane Austen adaptations to Captain America, Huxley's name surfaces as a shorthand for this seductive dystopia, reminding us that the brave new world is not a future to fear but a present we must resist.

## 30-Second Takeaways
- Huxley's dystopia controls through pleasure and distraction, not force—making it more insidious than Orwell's.
- The novel warns that comfort and stability can be chains, and that real freedom requires danger and suffering.
- Huxley's early career as a screenwriter (e.g., Jane Austen adaptations) shows his lifelong fascination with narrative and illusion.
- Modern attention economics echo the World State's methods: engineering desire to keep us docile and consuming.
- The phrase 'brave new world' has become a cultural shorthand for any society that trades depth for convenience.
