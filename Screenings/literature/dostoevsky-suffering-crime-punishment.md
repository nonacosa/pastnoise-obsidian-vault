## Core Thesis
Crime and Punishment is often called pro-suffering, but Dostoevsky does not bless pain; he exposes what happens when a mind tries to vault over conscience. Raskolnikov's crime begins as a theory about permissible exceptions and ends as isolation, illness, and the slow return of other people. The novel's serial publication in 1866 and its confessional lineage in Notes from Underground sharpen its warning: pride can become a cell, and suffering becomes human only when it turns us back toward the suffering we caused.

## 30-Second Takeaways
- The murder is less the crime than the theory that permits it: a bright abstraction used to silence another person's ordinary life.
- Sonia's family and the Marmeladovs show suffering as social fact, not noble idea; poverty damages bodies before it becomes philosophy.
- Dostoevsky's warning against nihilism is practical: when self-pride isolates a person, conscience returns as fever and dread.
- Notes from Underground supplies the confessional pattern: a self-lacerating intelligence that prefers cleverness to loving actual neighbors.
- The novel's pro-suffering reputation is misleading; it asks whether suffering can be redeemed through responsibility, not whether pain is good.
