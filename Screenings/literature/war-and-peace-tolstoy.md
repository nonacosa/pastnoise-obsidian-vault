> Tolstoy's War and Peace as a mirror for how we judge nations, leaders, and ourselves.

## Core Thesis
Tolstoy's War and Peace endures not as a war chronicle but as a philosophical provocation: history is made by ordinary people, not by great men. The novel's refusal to simplify Napoleon or the Russian cause mirrors our own struggle to understand failure—whether of empires or of individuals. Niall Ferguson credits Tolstoy with shaping how we see power and money; the Austrian case shows the cost of ignoring such lessons. The documentary asks: what happens when we treat history as a story we can master?

## 30-Second Takeaways
- Tolstoy's greatest impact lies in his insistence that history is shaped by countless small decisions, not by singular genius.
- Austria-Hungary's WWI failures—military blunders, ethnic strife, economic malaise—illustrate the cost of ignoring systemic fragility.
- Russian writers of the 19th century disagreed about Napoleon, revealing how national identity is forged through contested memory.
- Tolstoy's method for handling insults—restraint and perspective—applies as much to nations as to individuals.
- The novel's dual narrative and historical essays challenge readers to question the very nature of cause and effect.
