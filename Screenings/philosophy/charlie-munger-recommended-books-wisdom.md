# The Unfinished Curriculum

> Charlie Munger’s learning was not a phase but a daily discipline of borrowed eyes and stubborn reading.

## Core Thesis
Across Buffett and Munger’s unscripted annual meetings, mentorship across generations, and field seminars that pair sophomores with elders, lifelong learning appears as a practical ethic rather than a slogan. Munger’s example—reading widely, arguing plainly, revising judgments—joins the written record of his life and Berkshire’s culture. The sources converge on a simple discipline: stay teachable, seek younger teachers, and let evidence overrule ego. The tension is equally clear: conviction without curiosity hardens into ideology. It is less a triumph of memory than a habit of attention.

## 30-Second Takeaways
- Munger and Buffett’s unscripted meetings show learning as public practice: read constantly, test ideas aloud, and revise when facts disagree with reputation.
- Chip Conley’s reverse mentorship—asking younger colleagues to teach him—turns humility into a repeatable method rather than a gesture.
- Stanford’s field seminar pairs sophomores and elders in the Galapagos, proving learning accelerates when generations are forced to explain themselves.
- Munger’s blunt Bitcoin dismissal and Wells Fargo commentary reveal conviction’s edge: lifelong learning does not mean endless neutrality.
- The written Munger record—Tren Griffin’s peer-reviewed study and Buffett’s own words—frames lifelong learning as character, not credential.
