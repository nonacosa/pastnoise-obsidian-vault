## Core Thesis
Palmer Luckey’s Anduril turns virtual-reality ambition into defense hardware: rugged computers for military and public-sector buyers, autonomous systems, American-made laptops. The Bloomberg and TBPN moments show procurement as product launch; Rogan and Diamandis expose the cultural bargain between consumer appetite and strategic dependence. Scholarship on blitzscaling and military norms warns that venture tempo can rewrite warfare before publics deliberate, while older work on prediction reminds us that futures are contested. The question is not whether war becomes technical, but who signs for the update.

## 30-Second Takeaways
- Anduril’s rugged computer is a category claim: defense procurement recast as a product line for military and public-sector customers.
- Luckey’s warning about Chinese cars mirrors his defense argument: dependence is a strategic choice disguised as consumer convenience.
- Autonomous weapons are framed as ancient in logic, not novel in kind—the novelty is venture-capital speed and software iteration.
- The $90 billion valuation turns war into a market signal, while Anduril declines comment—public silence, private scale.
- Palmer Luckey’s Oculus lineage matters: the same interface ambition now targets battlespace perception rather than consumer entertainment alone.
